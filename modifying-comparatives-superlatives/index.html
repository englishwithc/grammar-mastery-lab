Measuring the gap.

A comparative tells you which one is more. It doesn't tell you by how much. These small words — much, slightly, nearly, by far — measure the gap. Choose by size, then check where the word sits.

01 — the big idea

How big is the gap?

Move A. The gap changes, and so does the sentence you need.

A
B
The scale isn't in metres or degrees. It's whatever property you're comparing: height, price, speed, difficulty, age.

The second question: where does the word go?

After the verb (predicative)

That tower is a lot taller.

Almost every modifier is happy here. This is the safe position.

Before the noun (attributive)

a much taller tower

Stricter. Some modifiers are blocked here and need a new structure.

Now do it yourself

Read the situation. Which group of words do you need?

Say it out loud first, then reveal.

Your phone cost 300. That one costs 900.
Big gap → much / far / a lot more expensive.
“That phone is much more expensive than mine.”
Two jackets: 80 euros and 78 euros.
Small gap → slightly / a little / a bit.
“This one is slightly more expensive.”
A weighs 20 kg. B weighs 45 kg.
A is far below B → not nearly as…as.
“A is not nearly as heavy as B.”

02 — big differences

Much, far, a lot

All three say the same thing: the gap is large. The difference is style and position.

  • This exam was much harder than the last one.
  • The new system is far more reliable than the old one.
  • She looks a lot happier today.
The one restriction: a lot can't sit before a noun. Use much / far there, or rebuild with a relative clause: a tower that is a lot taller.

Now do it yourself

Fix these three. One word, or one small restructure each.

This bag is very heavier than mine.
very heavier → much heavier (or far heavier / a lot heavier). “Very” cannot modify a comparative.
They live in a lot bigger house than us.
a lot bigger house → a much bigger house
or: a house that is a lot bigger
This version is more better than the first.
more better → much better. “Better” is already a comparative.

03 — small differences

Slightly, a little, a bit

The difference is real, but it's small. Often it's a difference that doesn't really matter.

  • The second test was slightly easier.
  • I feel a little better today.
  • The weather was a bit nicer than we expected.
Classroom shortcut: slightly is the safe choice before a noun. A little and a bit belong after the verb — or inside a longer phrase: a performance a bit longer than expected.

Why? “a bit” already contains the article a, so it clashes with the noun's own article.

Now do it yourself

Correct or not? Say yes or no, then check.

It was a bit longer film than I expected.
✗ → a slightly longer film / a film a bit longer than I expected
The soup was a little hotter than yesterday.
✓ Correct. It comes after the verb, so “a little” is fine.
We booked a slightly more expensive room.
✓ Correct. “Slightly” works before a noun.

04 — almost the same

Nearly as…as, almost as…as

The equative pattern is as + adjective + as. Add nearly or almost and you say: close to the same, but a little below.

A
the new material
B
steel

A sits just below B — the gap is tiny.

A useful tendency: nearly is common with numbers and measurements (nearly as tall as); almost is common with abstract qualities and manner (almost as important as, almost as quickly as). In positive sentences, the meaning is the same.

Now do it yourself

Nearly, almost, or neither?

The copy is ______ as good as the original.
Either: almost or nearly. Both are natural here.
It is not ______ as cold as yesterday.
Only not nearly as cold as. “not almost as” is ungrammatical.
He finished ______ as quickly as his brother.
almost as quickly as sounds most natural (manner). “Nearly” is possible but less common.

05 — a big gap downwards

Not nearly as…as, nowhere near as…as

This is one of the most useful spoken patterns in English. It replaces much less … than in everyday conversation.

Two strengths

FormMeaningRegister
not nearly as…asmuch less — strongneutral, emphatic
nowhere near as…asmuch less — very stronginformal, conversational
Never build it from “almost”. Teach it as one fixed chunk: not nearly as … as. It also stands alone: We are nowhere near finished.

Now do it yourself

Say the same thing the conversational way.

The new laptop is much slower than the old one.
“The new laptop is not nearly as fast as the old one.” Stronger: “nowhere near as fast as”.
Our flat is much smaller than theirs.
“Our flat is not nearly as big as theirs.”
The second trial was much less successful than the first.
“The second trial was not nearly as successful as the first.”

06 — clearly number one

By far + superlative

A superlative already says “number one”. By far adds: number one by a large distance.

the best candidate
the second best

The gap between number one and number two is what “by far” describes.

by far is flexible: by far the best or the best by far.
far and away is fixed: only before the.
Don't blend them: by far and wide, by far and away.

Now do it yourself

Where does it go?

It is the best solution ______. (by far)
✓ at the end: “It is the best solution by far.”
______ the most convincing explanation. (far and away)
✓ before “the”: “Far and away the most convincing explanation.”
She is the best by far and away.
✗ Choose one: She is by far the best. / She is far and away the best.

07 — ranked position

The second highest, the third fastest

Put an ordinal number between the and the superlative. “The second highest” means: exactly one thing is above it, and nothing else.

    Ties: if two trains arrive at the same time, they share a rank — the second earliest trains can mean a tied pair.

    Two readings: John climbed the third highest mountain can mean third in the world, or third among the mountains his group climbed. Context decides.

    Now do it yourself

    Say it with an ordinal.

    Everest is 8,849 m. K2 is 8,611 m. What is K2?
    K2 is the second highest mountain in the world.
    Runners: A 9.8 s, B 9.9 s, C 10.1 s. What is B?
    B is the second fastest runner.
    Fix the word order: “the highest second mountain”
    the second highest mountain — the ordinal goes after “the”.
