Grammar lab · B1 · conditionals

If things were different.

English has a trick for things that are not true: it takes the verb one step back in time. Do it once and you have the second conditional. Do it twice and you have the third.

01the one big idea

Take one step back

The past tense does not always mean past. Here it means not real. Click each step and watch the verb move backwards.

present simple first conditional
The pattern: real have → imaginary had → imaginary past had had. Each step is one tense further back. Nothing else changes.

over to you

Move each fact one step back.

Say the imaginary sentence out loud first, then reveal.

Fact: “I’m not tall.”
If I were taller, I would play basketball.
“am” → “were”. Two steps? No, one: present → past.
Fact: “I don’t speak French.”
If I spoke French, I would work in Paris.
“don’t speak” → “spoke”. The meaning is still now.
Fact: “I didn’t save any money last year.”
If I had saved some money, I would have bought a car.
This is the second step back: past → past perfect.

02not real now

The second conditional

One step back from the present. It describes:

If + past simple → would + base verb
trueI don’t live near the sea. imaginaryIf I lived near the sea, I would swim every day.
trueI can’t speak Japanese. imaginaryIf I spoke Japanese, I would work in Tokyo.
trueI don’t have a car. imaginaryIf I had a car, I would drive you home.

What it is used for

  • An imaginary present: If I lived by the sea, I would swim every day.
  • An unlikely future: If I won the lottery, I would buy a house. (Past tense here signals “probably won’t happen”.)
  • Advice: If I were you, I would apologise.
  • A polite no: If I had a car, I’d drive you — but I don’t.
Commas and contractions: if the if part comes first, use a comma. No comma when it comes second. In speech, would becomes ’d: I’d swim. In negative sentences: wouldn’t, didn’t.
One small choice: was or were? With I / he / she / it, the standard form is were: If I were you… In everyday speech many people say If I was you, but in exams, writing and job applications, use were.

over to you

Two tenses fight in each sentence. Fix them.

Write the correct version before you reveal.

If I would have a bigger flat, I would get a dog.
would have → had

Never put would in the if part.
If I would win, I would travel the world.
would win → won

If I am you, I would take the job.
am → were
If I were you, I would take the job.

03not real in the past

The third conditional

Two steps back. The moment has passed, so the situation can never change now. It is only in your head.

If + had + past participle → would have + past participle

The solid line is real. The dashed line is the third conditional.

Choose a real past action. The sentence appears.

Two hads? Yes. If I had had more time is correct — the first had is the past-perfect helper, the second is the verb have. In speech it often becomes If I’d had more time.

over to you

Regret or relief?

Decide first, then check the sentence.

“I didn’t set an alarm. I missed my flight.”
If I had set an alarm, I wouldn’t have missed my flight.
Regret. Note: “didn’t set” becomes positive “had set”.
“We left early. We didn’t get stuck in traffic.”
If we hadn’t left early, we would have got stuck in traffic.
Relief. Here the if part is the negative one.
“She didn’t study. She failed.”
If she had studied, she wouldn’t have failed.
Or: she would have passed. Both work.

05two times, one sentence

The mixed map

The if part and the result do not have to be in the same time. One story: Maria does not speak Spanish, and that changed two things.

result: now
result: past
if-part: now
if-part: past
How to choose: ask two questions. Is the if-part about now or about the past? Then is the result about now or about the past? The verbs follow the answers, not the other way round.

over to you

Which part is about now?

“If I hadn’t moved to Berlin, I would still work in Paris.”
if-part = past (moving). result = now (still working there).
So it is a mixed conditional.
“If he weren’t so shy, he would have spoken to her.”
if-part = now (he is shy — a permanent trait). result = past (he didn’t speak).
Also mixed, but the other way round.

06listening detail

’d — is it had or would?

Both contract to ’d. There is a simple test, and it is about where the ’d is.

Inside the if-part → had. In the result → would.
Still unsure? Look at the next word: ’d + past participle (known, left, seen) = had. ’d + base verb (go, help) or ’d have = would.

07optional · very formal

Dropping if

In formal writing, you can delete if and move had or were to the front. The meaning does not change at all — only the tone.

One strict rule: you cannot contract not after inversion. Hadn’t he told me, I would have been late. → Had he not told me, I would have been late.

over to you

Make it formal.

If we had known about the delay, we would have called you.
Had we known about the delay, we would have called you.
If she were the manager, she would change the rota.
Were she the manager, she would change the rota.
